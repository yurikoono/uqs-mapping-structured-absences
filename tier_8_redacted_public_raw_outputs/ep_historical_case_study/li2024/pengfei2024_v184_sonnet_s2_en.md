**Candidate 8: The radical clock experiment provides qualitative but not quantitative evidence for radical mechanism**

The paper uses the ring-opening of cyclopropylmethyl bromide (experiment 2, Fig. 5b) to demonstrate the involvement of a radical intermediate: [REDACTED: substantial verbatim quotation; see Tier 9] The ring-opened product 94 is reported at 53% yield, but the unrearranged cross-coupling product — which would also form if any radical is captured by Ni before ring-opening occurs — is not reported. In a standard radical-clock experiment, the ratio of rearranged to unrearranged product constrains the radical lifetime and, critically, whether the radical is caged within the Ni coordination sphere or freely diffusing in solution. The absence of the unrearranged product yield removes the ability to distinguish these scenarios from the paper's own mechanism. **Scope abstraction:**

The paper answered: *Does a nickel electrocatalytic system enable cross-electrophile coupling of two unactivated alkyl halides, achieving selective cross-coupling over homocoupling via a radical mechanism without stoichiometric external reductants?*

The abstract chemical form is: *Can a [transition metal electrocatalyst] preferentially couple two [electronically similar, intrinsically unreactive substrates] in a [cross-selective] manner by exploiting [stoichiometric asymmetry and differential metal affinity] rather than [inherent substrate reactivity differences]?*

**Transformations:**

1. *Biochemistry (same structure, different guise):* Can an enzyme (transition metal electrocatalyst analogue) couple two similarly activated nucleotides (electronically similar substrates) in a cross-selective manner by exploiting concentration asymmetry and differential active-site affinity, rather than differences in intrinsic reactivity? — "same structure in a different guise."

2. *Materials science (same structure, different guise):* Can an electrochemically driven radical process couple two structurally similar but unreactive polymer chain termini without reductant, using differential chain-end radical persistence (analogous to 1° vs. 2° radical addition rates) to control sequence selectivity? — "same structure in a different guise."

3. *General electrochemical catalysis framework (broadest reach):* When two substrates with similar electrochemical reduction potentials must be cross-coupled selectively, can stoichiometric asymmetry alone (one substrate in excess) substitute for inherent substrate reactivity differentiation, and what minimum selectivity factor is required? — "same structure in a different guise," broadened to a general design principle.

4. *Economics/policy (figurative similarity):* Can a [market mechanism] preferentially pair two [similarly competitive actors] in a cross-productive transaction by exploiting [supply asymmetry] rather than [inherent advantage differences]? — "figurative similarity only."

**Selection of broadest transformation:**

Transformation 3 has the broadest reach. It abstracts the core question from a specific Ni/alkyl-halide system to a general principle in electrochemical catalysis: the conditions under which stoichiometric asymmetry can substitute for substrate inherent differentiation as the source of cross-selectivity. This question applies across any electrochemically driven radical coupling where two substrates have overlapping reduction potentials (e.g., aryl halide cross-coupling, C–O bond formation, C–N bond formation), and its answer would define a design principle for a wide class of eXEC reactions. This is a framework_question about the conditions governing selectivity, not a tool_question about a specific catalyst.

The confirmed question, however, is not this broad abstraction but is faithful to the paper's actual research problem (whether a specific Ni electrocatalytic system enables eXEC of unactivated alkyl halides), which is a tool_question.
