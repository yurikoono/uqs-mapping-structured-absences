## Unanswered Questions and Unsupported Claims

---

### 1. Proposed catalytic intermediates are not observed in this system

The paper presents a three-step catalytic cycle involving diorganonickel complex **1**, haloorganonickel complex **2**, and a second diorgano complex **3** (eqs 2–4). Each step is supported by precedent drawn from *separate* organometallic systems (refs 11–13), none of which involves the specific Grignard/vinyl- or arylchloride substrates studied here. None of the proposed intermediates is isolated, detected spectroscopically, or kinetically characterized within this work.

**Evidence needed:** Isolation or in-situ spectroscopic observation (NMR, IR) of intermediates **1**, **2**, and/or **3** under the actual reaction conditions; kinetic studies showing rate laws consistent with the proposed cycle.

### 2. Anomalous cis-enrichment in stilbene synthesis is unaccounted for

Both *cis*- and *trans*-1,2-dichloroethylene deliver predominantly *cis*-stilbene (80:20 from *cis*; 43:57 from *trans*). No mechanism is proposed to explain why a *trans* starting material also gives a *cis*-enriched product, which is the more surprising result. The word "anomalously" signals that the authors recognised the anomaly without resolving it.

**Evidence needed:** Mechanistic experiments distinguishing syn/anti addition, isomerisation of a vinyl–nickel intermediate, or thermodynamic equilibration; labelling or crossover experiments; structural characterisation of any vinyl–nickel species.

### 3. The inference that cis geometry of the diorganonickel intermediate is the "first requisite" is not demonstrated

The ligand activity order ([NiCl₂(dpp)] > [NiCl₂(dpe)] > [NiCl₂(dmpe)] ≫ monodentate complexes) is interpreted as evidence that cis placement of the two organic groups in intermediate **3** is essential. The argument is purely structural and analogical: bidentate ligands enforce cis geometry, monodentate ligands may not. Whether the cis complex is the active species, whether reductive elimination is the rate-determining step, and whether *cis*-complex formation correlates with turnover are all unaddressed.

**Evidence needed:** X-ray or NMR structural confirmation that intermediate **3** adopts a *cis* geometry; rate measurements for reductive elimination as a function of ligand bite angle; comparison of a conformationally locked *trans* diorganonickel to confirm its inactivity.

### 4. Suppression of β-hydride elimination is claimed but not explained

The paper highlights that alkylmagnesium reagents bearing β-hydrogen atoms (e.g., *n*-BuMgBr, *n*-C₈H₁₇MgCl) give high isolated yields. This is presented as a significant advantage, with a footnote acknowledging the general instability of transition-metal alkyls bearing β-H. No mechanistic rationale is offered for why, in this Ni–phosphine system, β-hydride elimination is suppressed enough to allow productive cross-coupling.

**Evidence needed:** Detection or quantification of β-hydride elimination by-products (alkenes, alkanes) under the reaction conditions; deuterium-labelling experiments; comparison of reaction rates for β-H vs. non-β-H Grignard reagents to establish whether elimination is kinetically or thermodynamically disfavoured.

### 5. Restriction to sp² carbon halides is stated without mechanistic or experimental justification

The paper asserts categorically that only Csp² halides (vinylic and aromatic) are competent electrophiles. This is presented as an empirical boundary but is never rationalised, and no experiments with alkyl halides are reported to demonstrate their failure under these conditions. The contrast with Kochi's iron-catalysed coupling (which the paper acknowledges also works for "alkenyl halides only") is noted in passing but not explored.

**Evidence needed:** Explicit experiments with representative alkyl halides (primary, secondary) under condition A and B to confirm inactivity; kinetic or electrochemical data distinguishing oxidative addition rates for sp² vs. sp³ C–Cl bonds to this Ni(0) centre.

### 6. The superiority of diethyl ether over THF is asserted without comparative data

The claim that ether is "definitely superior" to THF is made without any tabulated comparison—no yield data, no rate data, and no side-product distribution in THF is provided.

**Evidence needed:** Side-by-side yield or conversion data for representative substrate pairs run in ether vs. THF under otherwise identical conditions; explanation of whether the difference is due to Grignard speciation, catalyst solubility, or coordination equilibria.

### 7. The gem-dichloroalkene case (Cl₂C=CH₂ → Ph₂C=CH₂) invokes two C–Cl activations without mechanistic discussion

The coupling of PhMgBr with vinylidene dichloride (Cl₂C=CH₂) gives 1,1-diphenylethylene in 82% yield. This product requires sequential activation of two C–Cl bonds, but the proposed catalytic cycle (eqs 2–4) is written for a single C–X bond per turnover. Whether the gem-dihalide undergoes two independent catalytic cycles, whether a dichloro intermediate is involved, or whether the mechanism diverges entirely is not addressed.

**Evidence needed:** Isolation or detection of a PhC(Cl)=CH₂ intermediate; stoichiometric experiments with one equivalent of PhMgBr; kinetic comparison of the two successive C–Cl bond activations.

### 8. Reported yields are acknowledged as sub-optimal, leaving the true synthetic scope undefined

A blanket footnote to Table I states that conditions are "not necessarily optimum," and several yields are measured by GLPC rather than as isolated material. The scope of the reaction—in terms of Grignard and halide substitution patterns, functional-group tolerance, and scale—therefore cannot be evaluated from the data presented.

**Evidence needed:** Optimised conditions for each substrate class; isolated yields for all entries; experiments probing functional-group compatibility (e.g., ester, nitrile, ketone substituents on either coupling partner).
