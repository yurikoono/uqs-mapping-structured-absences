## Unanswered Questions and Unsupported Claims

---

**1. The catalytic cycle is a working hypothesis derived by analogy, not demonstrated under catalytic conditions.**

The three-step cycle (eqs 2–4), including diorganonickel intermediates 1, 2, and 3, is inferred from stoichiometric reactions reported elsewhere (refs 11, 12) in different coordination environments. No intermediate is detected, isolated, or spectroscopically characterised in the actual Grignard/chloride/[NiCl₂(dpe)] system reported here.

Evidence needed: Direct observation of intermediates 1–3 by in-situ spectroscopy under catalytic conditions; kinetic profiling consistent with the proposed sequence; rate-law determination.

**2. The requirement for a cis-diorganonickel geometry is inferred from the ligand activity order but not mechanistically justified.**

The paper moves from the empirical ligand ranking to the structural conclusion that "cis configuration … is the first requisite of the catalyst," without explaining how bidentate phosphines enforce cis geometry, whether the relevant intermediate is the diorgano complex or the halo/organo complex, or why monodentate phosphines fail.

Evidence needed: Crystal-structure or solution NMR characterisation of diorganonickel intermediates bearing each ligand; computational modelling of the reductive elimination transition state as a function of bite angle.

**3. The anomalous, non-stereospecific cis-enriched stilbene outcome from both cis- and trans-1,2-dichloroethylene is reported but left without mechanistic interpretation.**

Both stereoisomers of 1,2-dichloroethylene yield cis:trans stilbene mixtures enriched in cis, contrary to simple oxidative-addition/reductive-elimination stereochemistry. No mechanism for this scrambling or for the cis enrichment is offered.

Evidence needed: Stereochemical labelling experiments; determination of whether isomerisation occurs at the oxidative-addition step, at the diorganonickel stage, or during reductive elimination; trapping of the vinyl-nickel intermediate.

**4. The exclusion of alkyl (sp³) halides is observed but not rationalised.**

The paper states that only sp² halides are viable substrates but does not address whether sp³ halides fail at the oxidative-addition step, through β-hydride elimination after insertion, or for some other reason.

Evidence needed: Attempted reactions with primary or secondary alkyl chlorides under identical conditions; mechanistic experiments (e.g., isotope effects, Hammett correlations) distinguishing oxidative addition from decomposition of the organonickel alkyl.

**5. The remarkable high reactivity of sp² chlorides is noted but unexplained.**

The paper flags the reactivity of aryl and vinyl chlorides—normally the least reactive sp² halides toward Ni(0)—as "one of the most remarkable features," yet gives no account of how the diphosphine ligand environment enables this.

Evidence needed: Comparative reactivity data across Cl, Br, and I for the same substrate pairs; kinetics of the oxidative-addition step with different halides; electronic analysis of the Ni(0)/Ni(II) couple as a function of ligand.

**6. The superiority of diethyl ether over THF is asserted without explanation.**

The preference is described as "definite" and contrasted with Tamura–Kochi iron catalysis, but no data are shown and no hypothesis is offered for the solvent effect (e.g., Grignard speciation, ligand dissociation, catalyst solubility, coordination).

Evidence needed: Yield comparisons across a series of solvents for identical reactions; spectroscopic or conductometric characterisation of catalyst and Grignard species in each solvent.

**7. Reaction conditions are explicitly stated as non-optimal, leaving the true scope and ceiling yields undetermined.**

The table footnote acknowledges that conditions are not optimised. Several yields in Table I (e.g., 76% for Ph-n-Bu, 80% for α-NpCH=CH₂) may understate the method's capability, and the substrate range tested may not represent the limit of the reaction.

Evidence needed: Systematic optimisation of catalyst loading, temperature, solvent ratio, and Grignard equivalents for representative substrate pairs; attempted but failed substrate classes.

**9. The black coloration during reaction is noted but not addressed.**

Example 1 states that a black mixture forms on heating, which is consistent with Ni(0) colloid or catalyst decomposition, but the paper does not discuss catalyst speciation, turnover number, or whether the black species is the active catalyst, a resting state, or a deactivation product.

Evidence needed: Characterisation of the black species (TEM, XPS, or in-situ UV-vis); catalyst turnover number and decomposition kinetics; determination of whether heterogeneous Ni(0) is catalytically competent.

**10. Corriu and Masse's concurrent Ni(acac)₂ work is acknowledged but not compared.**

The "Note Added in Proof" discloses that Corriu and Masse independently reported similar Ni-catalysed cross-coupling, yet no comparison of scope, selectivity, conditions, or mechanism between [NiCl₂(dpe)] and Ni(acac)₂ is made.

Evidence needed: Head-to-head comparison of catalyst performance for identical substrate pairs; discussion of which catalyst class is better suited to which substrate type.

**11. Corey's π-allylnickel and diorganocuprate reagents are cited as "excellent" but set aside without comparison.**

Footnote 7 acknowledges these stoichiometric reagents as excellent for selective cross-coupling, then explicitly excludes them from discussion because "our interest centers on a transition metal catalyst." No comparison of substrate scope, functional-group tolerance, cost, or selectivity is made.

Evidence needed: Side-by-side substrate comparison; discussion of where catalytic Ni-phosphine and stoichiometric cuprate/allylnickel are complementary or competing; functional-group compatibility data.

## Same-Root Groups

**SRG-1 — Catalytic intermediates undetected (shared root: no mechanistic evidence from the catalytic system itself)**
Items 1, 2, 8: The catalytic cycle (item 1), the cis-geometry requirement (item 2), and the absence of homo-coupling data (item 8) all trace to the same gap: the paper proposes a mechanism by analogy without any in-situ observation of intermediates or direct measurement of competing pathways under the reported conditions.

**SRG-2 — Stereooutcome unexplained (shared root: no data on the reductive-elimination or oxidative-addition stereochemistry)**
Items 2, 3: The cis-geometry claim (item 2) and the non-stereospecific cis-stilbene result (item 3) both derive from the absence of information about the stereochemical course at the Ni centre.

**SRG-3 — Halide reactivity and substrate scope (shared root: no electronic/kinetic data on oxidative addition)**
Items 4, 5: The restriction to sp² halides (item 4) and the unexplained high reactivity of chlorides (item 5) both require the same missing data: kinetics and electronics of the Ni(0) oxidative-addition step as a function of halide and hybridisation.

**SRG-4 — Reaction conditions undercharacterised (shared root: no systematic optimisation data)**
Items 6, 7: The solvent effect (item 6) and the acknowledged non-optimality of conditions (item 7) both stem from the preliminary, proof-of-concept character of the study.

**SRG-5 — Parallel and prior art not engaged (shared root: all uncontinued citations framing novelty)**
Items 10, 11, 12: Corriu–Masse Ni(acac)₂ (item 10), Corey cuprate/allylnickel (item 11), and Tamura–Kochi iron (item 12) are each cited to contextualise the contribution but none is substantively compared in scope, selectivity, or mechanism.
